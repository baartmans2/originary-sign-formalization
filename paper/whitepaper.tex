\section{Motivation and Background}

\subsection{The originary hypothesis}

Eric Gans's Generative Anthropology proposes that language, and with it the
distinctively human forms of culture, originated in a single kind of event: the
\emph{originary scene} \cite{Gans1981,Gans1993}. In Gans's account, a group of
hominids converge, through mimetic desire, on a single desirable object. Mutual
imitation intensifies their rivalry beyond what an animal dominance hierarchy can
contain, and the group faces a violent crisis. The crisis is deferred when the
gesture of appropriation is aborted. The aborted gesture, shared and reciprocally
understood, becomes the first sign: an \emph{ostensive} that designates the object
as a centre which no one may take. The scene concludes in the \emph{sparagmos},
the collective division of the object.

From the ostensive, Gans derives the further elementary forms of language in
sequence. The \emph{imperative} arises from an ostensive made in the absence of its
referent, which the hearer answers by supplying the missing object. The
\emph{declarative} arises when the imperative cannot be fulfilled, and the hearer
preserves the relation symbolically instead. Later work in the tradition, notably
by Adam Katz, develops related notions, among them the \emph{centre} toward which
the attention of a scene converges, and the \emph{firstness} of the participant who
defers first.

The ambition of the theory is captured in its name. It is \emph{generative}: it
claims to derive complex linguistic and cultural forms from a minimal beginning.
Gans presents the originary hypothesis as a minimal one, positing a single event
and asking what must follow from it \cite{Gans1993,Katz2007}.

\subsection{The problem: narrative content of uncertain necessity}

This work grows out of a long engagement with Gans's writings and those of Adam
Katz, and out of a persistent dissatisfaction with one feature of the account. The
originary scene is not only a logical structure. It is also a story, and the story
has specific content: mimetic desire as the source of convergence on the object,
mimetic rivalry as the source of crisis, the prospect of catastrophic violence as
what compels deferral, a sacred centre, and a sparagmos that resolves the scene by
division. This content is inherited in part from Ren\'e Girard's mimetic theory
\cite{Girard1977}. Each element is anthropologically suggestive, and each ties the
origin of language to themes the theory develops elsewhere, such as the sacred,
ethics and exchange.

It is not evident, however, which of these elements the derivation of the sign and
its forms actually requires. Several are open to objection on their own terms.
Violence among humans and other animals is common and rarely catastrophic; disputes
end through exhaustion or through hierarchy without producing signs. Refraining from
an object one wants is, on ordinary assumptions about preference, a losing strategy,
so a deferral that is supposed to be motivated by self-interest needs further
explanation. And a collective division of the object adds a stage whose necessity
for the emergence of reference is unclear. Much of the narrative therefore appears
arbitrary relative to what it is meant to explain.

The issue is not whether these elements are plausible. It is whether they are
\emph{load-bearing}. If the derivation depends on them, it inherits their
contingency and takes on the character of a just-so story. If it does not, it
should be possible to state the derivation without them, and doing so would show
what the generative core of the theory actually is.

\subsection{Aim: minimal conditions for generativity}

The present work attempts to separate that core from its narrative content. Its aim
is to identify a minimal set of assumptions under which it can be \emph{proved} that
symbolic structure arises from conditions in which it is absent, and to make every
assumption explicit. Generativity is given three precise senses:

\begin{description}[leftmargin=1.5em, style=nextline]
  \item[Existence.] There is a model of the assumptions in which the structure arises
    from a prior state that lacks it.
  \item[Necessity.] In every model of the assumptions, whenever the structure exists,
    it has a determinate origin and order.
  \item[Universality.] The operations that produce the first symbolic structures
    suffice to produce any finite structure of the relevant kind.
\end{description}

\noindent The aim is not to show that such emergence was inevitable, nor that it
happened in one particular way. It is to show that emergence is possible under weak
conditions, and that its form is constrained by those conditions.

\subsection{Why graphs}

The structure whose emergence is at issue is taken to be relational structure in
general, in the form of graphs. Any finite relational system can be represented as a
graph. Relations among more than two terms can be encoded by an incidence
construction, in which each relation instance becomes a node joined to its
arguments. The question of how language begins is therefore approached
structurally. Consider a world that contains physical objects and physical
relations among them, but no symbolic nodes. How can symbolic nodes, and relations
among them, arise in such a world? Physical objects are assumed. Symbolic nodes are
not.

This framing makes precise a specific hypothesis that motivated the work: that
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item the ostensive establishes a contextual relation, an edge;
  \item the imperative attempts to complete an unsatisfied relation;
  \item the declarative yields an independently representable symbolic object, a node.
\end{itemize}
One outcome of the formalization is a sharper version of this hypothesis. The first
symbolic node is best understood as a \emph{reified relation}: a relation that has
itself become something one can refer to. Relations between nodes, in turn, arise by
the same operation applied one level up.

\subsection{Rival orderings}

A further motivation is comparative. The sequence ostensive, imperative, declarative
is one reading among several. Other interpretations, for instance accounts that give
priority to the declarative, plausibly license themselves within their own axioms:
each ordering may be derivable once suitable assumptions are made. If so, the
substantive question is not which ordering is correct in the abstract. It is which
assumptions each ordering requires, and how plausible those assumptions are. A
framework in which every assumption is explicit allows such alternatives to be
compared on equal terms. The present work develops one such framework and records,
for each result, the assumptions on which it depends.

\subsection{Why machine verification}

Arguments in humanistic theory often rely on steps that are intuitively compelling
but unstated. Formalization in a proof assistant forces every assumption to be
written down and every inference to be checked. All results reported here are
formalized in the Lean~4 proof assistant \cite{deMoura2021} and checked in full, with
no unproven steps.

The process was itself informative. Several assumptions that the informal narrative
leaves implicit were exposed only when a proof failed without them. Among them:
\begin{itemize}[nosep]
  \item a kinematic principle linking a reproduced gesture to the object it was
    originally directed at;
  \item the requirement that one participant defer before another, which corresponds
    to Katz's firstness;
  \item the dependence of falsehood on the drift of signs away from their iconic
    origins;
  \item the reliance of affirmation, derived as the negation of a negation, on
    classical logic.
\end{itemize}
Formalization also makes it possible to say exactly which statements are derived
and which are merely definitions. The paper marks this distinction throughout.
